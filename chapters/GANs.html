Probability Density (\(p(x)\)) v/s Probability Distribution (\(\mathbb{P}(x)\))

Discrete Variable:

\[P(X = x) = p(x) \quad \text{(Probability Mass Function)}\] \[\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \sum_{x \in A} p(x)\]

Continuous Variable:

No Probability Mass Function exists for continous variables (Cant find probability at a single point), but

\[\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \int_A p(x) \, dx\]

i.i.d (Independent and Identically Distributed)

Identical:

If 2 R.V's is drawn from the same distribution, then it is said to be identical.

Independent:

If the outcome of one R.V does not affect the outcome of another R.V, then they are said to be independent.

Consider 2 random variables \(X_1, X_2\) that are independent.

Then:

\[P(X_1 = x_1 | X_2 = x_2) = P(X_1 = x_1)\]
Note: Whenever the term i.i.d is used, Law of Large Numbers is activated for that R.V. \[ \text{LLN: } \quad \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \xrightarrow{P} \mathbb{E}[x] \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \]

Convex Conjugate

Consider the graph given below.

Here, \( f(u) \) is some function(convex or non-convex) which we're interested in finding the convex conjugate of.

and now we pick an arbitrary slope t, and line l(u) = tu + c

to say that the line is convex conjugate, it must satisfy:

\[ l(u) \leq f(u) \quad \text{for all } u \] \[ tu + c \leq f(u) \quad \text{for all } u \] \[ c \leq f(u) - tu \quad \text{for all } u \]

now, our job is to minimize c

\[ c = \inf_{u} \left\{ f(u) - tu \right\} \]

or,

\[ -c = \sup_{u} \left\{ tu - f(u) \right\} \]

We can write convex conjugate as a function of t:

\[ f^*(t) = \sup_{u} \left\{ tu - f(u) \right\} \]
Note: We've used the supremum (sup) instead of maximum (max) because the maximum may not be attained if \( f \) is not continuous or does not have a maximum value.

Ex: Consider the function \( f(x) \) mapped in the range \([0, 1)\) and \( f(x) = 0 \) for all \( x \in [0, 1) \).

\[max_{x \in [0, 1)} \{ f(x) \} = undefined\] \[sup_{x \in [0, 1)} \{ f(x) \} = 1\]

Generative Adversarial Networks

Let's derive the convex conjugate of \( f(u) = u \log u - (u+1)\log(u+1) \)

We want to find its convex conjugate, defined as: \[ f^*(t) = \sup_{u > 0} \left\{ tu - u \log u + (u+1)\log(u+1) \right\} \] Step 1: Find the critical point
Take the derivative of the expression inside the supremum with respect to u and set it to 0: \[ \frac{d}{du} \left( tu - u \log u + (u+1)\log(u+1) \right) = t - (\log u + 1) + (\log(u+1) + 1) = 0 \] Simplifying this, we get the relationship between t and u at the optimum: \[ t = \log u - \log(u+1) \] (Note: Since \( u > 0 \), the ratio \( \frac{u}{u+1} < 1 \), which implies \( t < 0 \).)

Step 2: Simplify the objective function
Instead of solving for u right away, substitute \( t = \log u - \log(u+1) \) directly into the original supremum expression: \[ f^*(t) = u(\log u - \log(u+1)) - u \log u + (u+1)\log(u+1) \] Expand the terms: \[ f^*(t) = u \log u - u \log(u+1) - u \log u + u \log(u+1) + \log(u+1) \] Notice how beautifully almost everything cancels out, leaving just: \[ f^*(t) = \log(u+1) \] Step 3: Solve for u+1 in terms of t
Now we just need to express \( \log(u+1) \) in terms of t. Using our relationship from Step 1: \[ t = \log\left(\frac{u}{u+1}\right) \] \[ e^t = \frac{u}{u+1} \] We can rewrite the right side as: \[ e^t = 1 - \frac{1}{u+1} \] Rearranging to solve for u+1: \[ \frac{1}{u+1} = 1 - e^t \implies u+1 = \frac{1}{1 - e^t} \] Step 4: Final Substitution
Substitute u+1 back into our simplified expression for \( f^*(t) \): \[ f^*(t) = \log\left(\frac{1}{1 - e^t}\right) \] Using the property of logarithms \( \log(1/x) = -\log(x) \), we get the final answer: \[ \boxed{ f^*(t) = -\log(1 - e^t) } \qquad \text{for } t < 0 \]









Training GANs





Conversion of GAN loss to BCE

We now convert the GAN loss to Binary Cross Entropy Loss version (for training purpose)

\[ \mathcal{J}_\text{GAN} = \mathbb{E}_{p_{x}} [\log D_{w}(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}} [\log (1 - D_{w}(g_{\theta}(z)))] \]

and we know, BCE Loss function for a single sample is:

\[ \mathcal{L_\text{BCE}} = - \left [y \log(\hat{y}) + (1 - y) \log(1 - \hat{y}) \right ] \]

where, \(y\) is the true label {0,1} and \(\hat{y}\) is the predicted probability [0,1].


First, let's derive the Loss function of the Discriminator, viz divided into 2 parts:

1. Loss for real images:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{real}} &= BCE(1, D_w(x)) \\[4pt] &= -\left[ 1 \cdot \log D_w(x) + (1-1)\cdot \log(1-D_w(x)) \right] \\[4pt] &= -\log D_w(x) \end{aligned} \]

2. Loss for fake images:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{fake}} &= BCE(0, D_w(g_\theta(z))) \\[4pt] &= -\left[ 0 \cdot \log D_w(g_\theta(z)) + (1-0)\cdot \log(1-D_w(g_\theta(z))) \right] \\[4pt] &= -\log\left(1-D_w(g_\theta(z))\right) \end{aligned} \]

Now, the total loss for the Discriminator is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_D &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{real}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{fake}} \\[6pt] &= -\log D_w(x) - \log\left(1-D_w(g_\theta(z))\right) \\[6pt] &= BCE(1, D_w(x)) + BCE(0, D_w(g_\theta(z))) \\[6pt] \end{aligned} \]

and across B1 samples,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_D &= -\frac{1}{B_1} \sum_{i=1}^{B_1} \left[ \log D_w(x^{(i)}) + \log\left(1-D_w(g_\theta(z^{(i)}))\right) \right] \end{aligned} \] \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_D &= -\frac{1}{B_1} \sum_{i=1}^{B_1} \left[ BCE(1, D_w(x^{(i)})) + BCE(0, D_w(g_\theta(z^{(i)}))) \right] \end{aligned} } \]

Now, let's derive the Loss function for the Generator:

Since the generator receives noise,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_G &= BCE(0, D_w(g_\theta(z))) \\[4pt] &= -\left[ 0 \cdot \log D_w(g_\theta(z)) + (1-0)\cdot \log(1-D_w(g_\theta(z))) \right] \\[4pt] &= -\log(1-D_w(g_\theta(z))) \end{aligned} \]

and across B2 samples,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_G &= -\frac{1}{B_2} \sum_{i=1}^{B_2} \log\left(1-D_w(g_\theta(z^{(i)}))\right) \end{aligned} \] \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_G &= -\frac{1}{B_2} \sum_{i=1}^{B_2} BCE(0, D_w(g_\theta(z^{(i)}))) \end{aligned} } \]
Generator Trick

In practice, we do not use the original above loss for the Generator.

But Why??

Initially, the Generator outputs random noise & the Discriminator quickly learns to distinguish real images from random noise

\[ D_w(g_\theta(z)) \approx 0 \quad \text{for all } z \]

The main job of the Generator is:

\[ \underset{\theta}{\arg\min}\;\mathcal{L}_G \]

Now,

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_G}{\partial \hat{x}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{x}}\log(1-\hat{x}) = -\frac{1}{1-\hat{x}} \]

When \( \hat{x} \approx 0 \), the gradient becomes very small, leading to vanishing gradients and slow learning for the Generator.

So, instead of minimizing \( -\log(1-\hat{x}) \) term viz numerically unstable, we maximize \( \log \hat{x} \) term:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_G &= -\frac{1}{B_2} \sum_{i=1}^{B_2} \log D_w(g_\theta(z^{(i)})) \\[4pt] &= -\frac{1}{B_2} \sum_{i=1}^{B_2} BCE(1, D_w(g_\theta(z^{(i)}))) \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, our new loss function for the Generator is:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_G &= -\frac{1}{B_2} \sum_{i=1}^{B_2} BCE(1, D_w(g_\theta(z^{(i)}))) \end{aligned} } \]

Interpretation of GAN as Classifier-Guided Generative Sampler






Mode Collapse Problem
  • Ideally, different noise inputs should generate different samples:
\[ z_1 \neq z_2 \implies G(z_1) \neq G(z_2) \]
  • In mode collapse, many different noise inputs produce nearly the same output:
\[ z_1 \neq z_2 \quad \text{but} \quad G(z_1) \approx G(z_2) \]
  • The generator distribution covers only a few modes of the real data distribution and ignores the general distribution:
\[ p_\theta(x) \subset p_{x}(x) \]
  • Result: less diversity, repeated samples, and missing modes.

Example: after training our vanilla-GAN, it looks like our generator picked up 9 as it's main distribution and the Discriminator wasn't able to generalize it.
